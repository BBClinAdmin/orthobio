Coding and billing

PRP and BMAC as self-pay services: why insurance is hard, and how to run them.

Rob Pickels, MS. Updated November 2026.

Why PRP and BMAC are often self-pay services.

If you're adding platelet-rich plasma (PRP) or bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) to your practice, one of the first questions is how to bill for them. Four facts set the answer:

  • PRP is reported with Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 0232T, a Category III code (one of the temporary codes for new and emerging services) with no national payment amount. [3, 4]
  • Medicare's national policy covers PRP only for chronic diabetic wounds that won't heal. [5]
  • At least one regional Medicare contractor, one of the companies that process Medicare claims, has a written policy of not covering PRP for musculoskeletal injuries and joint conditions. [6]
  • Two large national commercial payers call PRP unproven in their coverage policies. [1, 2]

BMAC has only just been given a code of its own, 1095T, and Medicare lists it, like 0232T, as priced by each contractor rather than nationally. [3, 4]

That's why Boulder Biologics, the clinic we run, is a self-pay clinic. For a practice adding PRP and BMAC, the job is to run them correctly as self-pay services: the right Medicare paperwork for Medicare patients, a Good Faith Estimate for everyone paying out of pocket (a written estimate of expected charges that federal law requires), and the same records for every patient. [10]

In this article we'll explain why billing insurance for these procedures is so hard, then walk through how to run a self-pay program, step by step.

Why billing insurance is hard.

Three things stack up against a claim: the codes, Medicare's policies, and commercial payers' policies.

The codes have no set price

Most CPT codes carry relative value units (RVUs), and Medicare's fee schedule turns those units into a payment amount. [4] 0232T is a Category III code, and the American Medical Association (AMA), which maintains CPT, says Category III codes carry no RVUs: payment "is based on the policies of payers and not on a yearly fee schedule." [3] The code includes the whole PRP service, "including image guidance, harvesting and preparation when performed." [1, 2] Medicare's 2026 fee schedule lists it with status C, "Contractors price the code," which means each contractor sets a price case by case after reviewing the documentation. [4]

BMAC is newer still. The AMA has added Category III code 1095T for harvesting, processing, and injecting bone marrow aspirate concentrate, and Medicare lists it as contractor-priced too. [3, 4] The AMA's own file shows two different effective dates for it, so we'd check the date with your coder.

Medicare's national policy covers wounds

Medicare decides coverage at two levels. A National Coverage Determination (NCD) applies across the country. Where there's no national policy on a use, the regional contractors decide, and each can publish its own Local Coverage Determination (LCD). [5, 6]

For PRP, NCD 270.3 covers autologous PRP, made from the patient's own blood, "for the treatment of chronic non-healing diabetic wounds," and says nothing about musculoskeletal use. [5] At least one contractor has addressed that gap. Noridian Healthcare Solutions' LCD L39058 states: "This is a non-coverage policy for all Platelet Rich Plasma injections and/or applications as a means of managing musculoskeletal injuries and/or joint conditions." [6]

Two commercial payers' policies call PRP unproven

UnitedHealthcare's commercial policy lists platelet-rich plasma as "unproven and not medically necessary for any condition or indication." [1] Cigna's policy names 0232T and calls it "experimental, investigational, or unproven" for "ANY condition or indication." [2] That's two policies, not the whole market, but they show the pattern.

Put together, a claim for PRP in a knee or a tendon starts with a code that has no set price and, with these payers, a written policy against coverage. BMAC's code is newer and is priced contractor by contractor. [3, 4] That's the case for planning the program as self-pay from day one, with the paperwork that makes self-pay correct.

How to run PRP and BMAC as self-pay.

Self-pay takes more than charging the patient directly. Federal rules set what each patient must receive before the procedure, and which rules apply depends on how the patient is covered. Getting that right every time is a front-desk process as much as a clinical one.

1. Ask how the patient is covered, before you schedule

Every later step depends on the answer. The Good Faith Estimate rule itself starts with the scheduling provider asking whether the patient has insurance. [10] We'd make the question part of booking every PRP or BMAC visit, and record the answer.

2. Original Medicare: the paperwork depends on your Medicare status

Original Medicare is the federal program itself, as distinct from the private Medicare Advantage plans in step 3. What you give an Original Medicare patient depends on each clinician's own standing with Medicare, not the practice's. [13] If you already bill Medicare, you're enrolled, and the Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN) is your path. If you've opted out, meaning you've formally told Medicare, in writing, that you won't bill it, a private contract takes its place.

If you're enrolled in Medicare: the ABN, form CMS-R-131 from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), is given to Original Medicare patients when you expect Medicare to deny payment. [7] It tells the patient, before the service, that Medicare may not pay, which lets you hold the patient responsible for payment if it doesn't. [7, 9] The CMS manual says that when Medicare considers a service experimental, "the beneficiary must be given an ABN." [8] You deliver it before the service, give the patient time to decide, and keep a copy. [9] On the form, the patient chooses an option. Under Option 2, they take the service and ask you not to bill Medicare, and CMS states that honoring that choice does not violate the mandatory claim submission rules, which otherwise require clinicians to send Medicare a claim for covered services. [8] Please download the current form from the CMS website rather than reusing an old copy.

If you've opted out of Medicare: CMS tells opted-out physicians and practitioners not to use the ABN. [12] Instead, each Medicare patient signs a private contract before treatment, agreeing to pay your charge and not to send a claim to Medicare, and you keep the signed original. [13] You opt out by filing an affidavit with your Medicare contractor, and the opt-out runs for two years, renewing unless you cancel it. It applies to every Medicare patient you treat, except in emergency or urgent care. Medicare Advantage plans can't pay you either. [13]

If you've never enrolled and never opted out: please talk to health care counsel or your Medicare contractor before you treat Medicare patients with PRP or BMAC.

3. Medicare Advantage and commercial plans: check the plan's rules

The ABN isn't used for Medicare Advantage patients. [8] Their plan's own rules apply, so we'd check them before you quote a self-pay price. For a patient with commercial insurance, your contract with that payer decides whether you may bill its members directly for a service the plan doesn't cover. A patient who chooses not to have a claim sent to their plan counts as self-pay for the Good Faith Estimate. [10]

4. Everyone paying out of pocket: give a Good Faith Estimate

Under the No Surprises Act, the federal law on surprise medical bills, uninsured and self-pay patients get a written Good Faith Estimate of the expected charges for their care. [10] How soon you send it depends on when the visit is booked:

  • within one business day, when the service is booked three to nine business days ahead
  • within three business days, when it's booked ten or more business days ahead, or within three business days of the patient asking for one

If the planned services change, you send a new estimate no later than one business day before the visit. [10]

The estimate lists each expected item and service with its code and expected charge, the providers involved, and the required disclaimers, including that it isn't a contract and that the patient may dispute a bill that runs well over it. It has to be in a form the patient can save and print. [10] If a provider's bill comes in at least $400 over the estimate, the patient can use the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process, in which an independent reviewer decides what the patient owes. [11]

5. Set your prices once, and quote them the same way

Because the estimate lists expected charges code by code, a fixed price for each service keeps every estimate consistent and quick to produce. [10]

6. Record every patient's paperwork the same way

We'd keep the signed ABN or private contract for each Original Medicare patient [9, 13] and a copy of each Good Faith Estimate, filed the same way every time. We find it is best to automate this process as much as possible.

What to confirm before you start.

Before your first self-pay PRP or BMAC patient, we'd confirm these five things:

  • Each clinician's Medicare status: enrolled, opted out, or never enrolled
  • Your Medicare contractor's PRP policy, which you'll find in the Medicare Coverage Database, the CMS online search for national and local coverage policies
  • Each contracted commercial payer's PRP and BMAC policy, and what your contract says about billing members for services the plan doesn't cover
  • The codes your estimates will list, including whether any other codes may be reported alongside 0232T, and when 1095T takes effect
  • Who gives the ABN or private contract and the Good Faith Estimate, when, and where the copies are kept
